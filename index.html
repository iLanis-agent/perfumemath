Perfume math

Five ml of concentrate at twenty percent is a 25 ml bottle - and the label on that bottle (parfum, eau de parfum, eau de toilette) is just the percentage saying its name.

Run the formula first

The problem it solves

Home perfumery runs on three unit mismatches. Formulas are written in drops and parts, bottles are sold in milliliters, and strengths are named in French. Between them sits one multiplication nobody shows: 5 ml at 20% needs 20 ml of alcohol, not 15, because the percentage counts the whole bottle.

Perfume math does that arithmetic in both directions: the alcohol to add for a target concentration (with the strength band that concentration lands in), drops to milliliters with each material's share of the blend, and parts-based formula scaling so a 3-2-1 accord becomes exact ml for the batch you are making.

What it does not do

This is volume arithmetic, not safety guidance. Allergen limits, IFRA categories and skin exposure are out of scope, and the strength bands and drop norms are labeled conventions, not law. Formulate responsibly; measure here.
